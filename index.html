About Me

The engineer who kept asking what the numbers meant.

I came to Georgia Tech already a little ahead. Through dual enrollment I finished Calculus I and II, English Composition I and II, Introduction to Computing in Python, and introductory mechanics at Georgia Gwinnett College and Georgia State University while still in high school. Taking college courses early taught me how to manage my own schedule, ask for help before I fall behind, and hold myself to a college standard before anyone required it.

I enrolled in Computer Engineering because I liked logic, math, and building things that work. My first semester gave me real experience with that work. In ECE 2020 I designed combinational logic circuits from truth tables. In Physics 2212 I measured charge on strips of tape and watched a capacitor charge in an RC circuit. I learned a lot, and I also learned something about myself: the part I enjoyed most was rarely the hardware. It was the analysis, the moment of taking messy measurements and turning them into a model I could trust and a decision I could defend.

My macroeconomics class made that clearer. Building graphs of GDP expenditure shares from the Penn World Table was the assignment I thought about after it was due. I realized I want to spend my career on the systems behind economic decisions: how capital moves, how risk is priced, and how a company should allocate limited resources.

That is why I am changing my major to Industrial Engineering. ISyE at Georgia Tech is built on optimization, probability, statistics, and operations research, which are the same tools financial and quantitative analysts use every day. Switching is a correction, not a retreat, and this course is where I made it. ECE 1100 asked me to explore honestly, and the honest answer pointed me toward ISyE.

Career Goals

From first-year engineer to quantitative analyst

My long-term goal is to work in finance as a financial analyst or quantitative analyst. Financial analysts study a company's performance and the market around it to guide investment and budgeting decisions. Quantitative analysts go further into the math, building statistical and optimization models to price securities, measure risk, and test trading strategies. Both roles reward the same mix of skills: strong probability and statistics, programming, and the ability to explain what a model says to someone who has to act on it.

I do not plan to attend graduate school, so my undergraduate years have to carry the full weight of preparing me. My plan is to graduate in Spring 2029, a year early, by using my transfer credit and taking classes every summer. That timeline is ambitious, so each semester has a specific job.

Close the skill gaps. ISyE gives me the quantitative core. I will add financial skills on my own: Excel financial modeling, accounting basics, and Python for data analysis, building on the programming I learned in CS 1301.

Build a professional network. Joining a business fraternity, attending Georgia Tech career fairs, and reaching out to alumni in finance will help me find the paths I cannot see from a classroom.

Get real experience early. A Summer 2027 internship is my next milestone. Each internship after that should move closer to the analyst work I want to do full time.
