Featured · ECE 1100 Discovery Project · In planning

Breadboard Light-Activated Night Light

A circuit that turns an LED on when the room gets dark and off when it is bright, using a photoresistor, a transistor, and a 9V battery. No code involved.

The Problem

Most lights only need to be on some of the time. A night light that switches itself on when the room gets dark saves energy and removes a small daily chore. It is also one of the clearest examples of a circuit making a decision on its own: a sensor measures the world, and the circuit responds without any code. For my first ECE project I wanted something physical that I built with my own hands, small enough to finish in a semester but still using real components. My design goal was simple: the LED turns on when I cover the sensor and turns off when the sensor is in bright light.

The Design

The circuit has two parts. The first is a voltage divider made from a fixed resistor and a photoresistor, a component whose resistance drops in bright light and rises in the dark. The point between them connects to the base of an NPN transistor (a 2N2222). In bright light the photoresistor's resistance is low, so it pulls the base voltage down and the transistor stays off. When the room gets dark, its resistance climbs, the base voltage rises past about 0.7 volts, and the transistor switches on. The second part is the output: when the transistor turns on, current flows from the battery through a current-limiting resistor and the LED, and the LED lights up. The transistor works as an electronic switch, the same idea that sits inside every logic gate I studied in ECE 2020.

The Process

I started by watching a beginner tutorial on photoresistor night lights and studying beginner projects on Arduino Project Hub to learn how the parts fit together. I used Georgia Tech's Interdisciplinary Design Commons (the Hive) and the ECE Instructional Labs to get parts and borrow a breadboard and multimeter. My parts list is a breadboard, a photoresistor, an LED, a 2N2222 transistor, resistors, a 9V battery with a connector, and jumper wires. My build plan is to place the transistor, wire the voltage divider to its base, add the LED and its resistor, and connect the battery last. Then I will use the multimeter to measure the photoresistor's resistance in light and in shade and to check the voltage at the transistor's base.

My biggest challenge so far has been getting the materials. Collecting every part is taking longer than I expected, which has pushed my build behind the timeline in my original pitch. It taught me an early lesson about engineering projects: sourcing parts is part of the schedule, and it needs extra time planned in from the start.

Current Status

The build has not started yet. Because gathering the materials is taking longer than planned, the project is still in the planning stage. Once I have every part and assemble the circuit, I will test it against my design goal: the LED should turn on when I cover the photoresistor and turn off when the sensor is in bright light. I will record the photoresistor's resistance and the transistor's base voltage in both conditions with a multimeter, and update this page with photos and results before the Discovery Project Showcase.

What I Learned

So far this project has taught me to read a schematic and plan a build around it, and finishing it will give me hands-on practice building a circuit on a breadboard and checking it with a multimeter instead of guessing. It also connected my classes. Physics 2212 explained the voltage, current, and resistance behind the voltage divider, and ECE 2020 showed me that transistors acting as switches are the building blocks of digital logic. Planning a circuit where one transistor makes a decision has already made those lectures more concrete.

What's Next

I would add a potentiometer in place of the fixed resistor so the brightness level that triggers the light can be adjusted. A larger next step is rebuilding the project with an Arduino, which could read the light level as a number, log it over time, and control a brighter light.
